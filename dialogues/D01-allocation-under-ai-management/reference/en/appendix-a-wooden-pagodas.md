# Appendix A — Japanese wooden pagodas and the Hōryūji carpenters

*D01 · Reference edition · English*

Part IV draws on a body of craft knowledge that is familiar to most Japanese readers and
almost unknown elsewhere. This appendix supplies the background a reader needs to follow the
argument. It is written for a reader who has never seen a Japanese pagoda.

---

## A.1 What a pagoda is

A pagoda (塔, *tō*) is a Buddhist reliquary tower. It descends from the Indian *stupa*, a
domed mound built to hold relics of the Buddha. As Buddhism travelled through China and the
Korean peninsula to Japan in the sixth century, the stupa was transformed into a multi-storey
tower, and in Japan it was built almost always in **wood**. Japanese pagodas usually have three
or five storeys (三重塔 *sanjū-no-tō*, 五重塔 *gojū-no-tō*). The tower is not a building one
lives or worships in. It is a monument whose function is to mark and house the relic, and the
relic chamber is associated with the central pillar.

Several features are unfamiliar to a reader used to masonry towers.

- **Timber.** The principal material is *hinoki* (Japanese cypress, *Chamaecyparis obtusa*), a
  straight-grained, rot-resistant conifer.
- **Joinery.** Members are connected mainly by fitted joints cut into the timber — splices
  (継手 *tsugite*) that lengthen a member and connections (仕口 *shiguchi*) that join members at
  an angle — so that the structure is a large assembly of interlocking pieces that can be taken
  apart and repaired.
- **Deep eaves.** Each storey carries a broad, heavy roof projecting far beyond the walls,
  supported by stacked bracket sets (組物 *kumimono*). The weight of the eaves is part of how the
  structure is balanced.
- **Stacked storeys.** The storeys are not tied to one another by continuous columns. Each sits
  on the one below, so that in motion the storeys can shift slightly relative to each other.
- **The central pillar** (心柱 *shinbashira*). A single tall mast, usually *hinoki*, rises
  through the centre from a foundation stone to the bronze finial (相輪 *sōrin*) at the top. In
  many pagodas it is not rigidly attached to the surrounding storeys.
- **Pent roofs** (裳階 *mokoshi*). Some pagodas carry a smaller auxiliary roof below each main
  roof. The pagodas of Yakushiji have them, which is why these three-storey pagodas appear to
  have six storeys.

## A.2 Why they stand

Japan is one of the most earthquake-prone countries in the world, yet tall wooden pagodas have
very rarely collapsed in earthquakes. Records suggest only two have done so in about 1,400 years.
Why is still studied and debated. Explanations proposed since around 1900 include friction
damping in the wooden joints, the flexibility of the frame, the effect of scale, and the
behaviour of the central pillar, which may sway out of phase with the storeys and so damp their
motion. Engineers have borrowed the idea: the vibration-control system of the Tokyo Skytree is
called a *shinbashira* after the pagoda's central pillar. One engineering study ends by noting
that the most effective factor in a pagoda's survival may have been the people who maintained it
for 1,300 years — a remark that applies also to Part IV.

## A.3 Hōryūji and Yakushiji

**Hōryūji** (法隆寺), at Ikaruga near Nara, holds the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the
world: its five-storey pagoda and its Golden Hall (金堂 *kondō*). Dendrochronology reported in
2001 found that the *hinoki* of the pagoda's central pillar was felled in 594 CE.

**Yakushiji** (薬師寺), in Nara, was the first Japanese temple laid out with a pair of pagodas
flanking its main hall. Fires destroyed the original compound except for the **East Pagoda**, an
original three-storey pagoda with pent roofs, praised as "frozen music" for the rhythm of its
roofs. The **West Pagoda** burned in 1528 and stood as bare foundation stones for over four
centuries. It was rebuilt in 1981, in the style of the Hakuhō period (late seventh to early eighth
century), under the direction of the master carpenter Nishioka Tsunekazu, after the Golden Hall
had been rebuilt in 1976. The work took about three years. The West Pagoda was deliberately built
slightly taller than the East Pagoda, on the expectation that as the timber shrinks and settles
over the centuries the two will come to the same height. Its vivid vermilion and green show how the
weathered East Pagoda once looked. The East Pagoda itself completed a full dismantling repair of
about twelve years around 2020–21.

## A.4 Nishioka Tsunekazu and the oral transmissions

**Nishioka Tsunekazu** (西岡常一, 1908–1995) was born into a family that had served Hōryūji as
carpenters for generations, and was trained from childhood by his grandfather. He is often called
the last master carpenter of Hōryūji, and was known as "the demon of Hōryūji" for his
uncompromising standards. He led work in the great Shōwa-era repair of Hōryūji, the rebuilding of
the three-storey pagoda of Hōrinji (法輪寺, completed 1975), and the restoration of Yakushiji's
Golden Hall and West Pagoda. His only live-in apprentice, **Ogawa Mitsuo** (小川三夫), founded the
temple-carpentry company Ikaruga Kōsha (鵤工舎) in 1977 and has trained many carpenters since. The
book *Ki no inochi, ki no kokoro* (『木のいのち木のこころ』, "The life of wood, the heart of wood"),
by Nishioka, Ogawa and the writer Shiono Yonematsu, records their teaching.

A temple carpenter is called a *miyadaiku* (宮大工), and the master who leads a project a *tōryō*
(棟梁). The Hōryūji carpenters passed their principles down as **oral transmissions** (口伝
*kuden*). Those relevant to D01:

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 木を買わず山を買え | *ki o kawazu yama o kae* | Do not buy timber; buy the mountain. Build one hall or tower from the trees of one mountain. |
| 木は生育の方位のままに使え | *ki wa seiiku no hōi no mama ni tsukae* | Use timber in the orientation in which it grew: trees from the south of the mountain on the south of the tower, from the north on the north. |
| 峠および中腹の木は構造材、谷の木は造作材に | — | Trees from ridges and upper slopes (strong, strongly twisted) for structural members; trees from valleys for finish work. |
| 堂塔の木組みは寸法で組まず木の癖で組め | *dōtō no kigumi wa sunpō de kumazu ki no kuse de kume* | Assemble halls and towers not by dimensions but by the twist of each timber. |
| 木の癖組みは工人たちの心組み | — | The assembly of timbers by their twist is the assembly of the craftsmen's hearts. |
| 工人たちの心組みは匠長が工人への思いやり | — | The assembly of the craftsmen's hearts is the master's consideration for his craftsmen. |

Two details make the second and fourth teachings concrete. When south-side trees are placed on the
south, the south columns — the front of the building, facing the sun — show many knots, because
trees grow branches on their sunny side. At Hōryūji and Yakushiji the front columns are indeed
knotty while the north columns show almost none. And on *kuse*: a trunk that grew under a
prevailing wind tends to twist one way, and the tendency persists after felling. Nishioka observed
that even thousand-year-old timber, when taken apart, still twists in the direction it always did,
and he called a timber's *kuse* its "heart". Setting a right-twisting member against a
left-twisting one lets the two restrain each other, so that the assembly as a whole stays true.
This is *kusegumi* (癖組み), "assembly by twist".

## A.5 Glossary

| Term | Japanese | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *miyadaiku* | 宮大工 | carpenter specialising in temples and shrines |
| *tōryō* | 棟梁 | master carpenter leading a project |
| *kuden* | 口伝 | oral transmission of a craft's principles |
| *kuse* | 癖 | a timber's habitual twist or set; by extension a person's characteristic tendency |
| *kusegumi* | 癖組み | assembling members so that their twists cancel |
| *hinoki* | 檜 | Japanese cypress, the principal structural timber |
| *shinbashira* | 心柱 | central pillar of a pagoda |
| *mokoshi* | 裳階 | auxiliary pent roof below a main roof |
| *sōrin* | 相輪 | bronze finial at the top of a pagoda |
| *tsugite*, *shiguchi* | 継手・仕口 | splice joints and angle joints cut into the timber |
| *kumimono* | 組物 | bracket sets supporting the eaves |

## A.6 Why this matters to D01

Three features of this tradition carry the argument of Part IV.

1. **The unit of judgment is the whole structure, not the single member.** A timber is never "good"
   or "bad" in itself; it is right or wrong *for a place*.
2. **A member's tendency is read from where and how it grew, and shows under load.** The carpenter
   reads the mountain before felling and watches the timber as it dries.
3. **The tradition explicitly extends from timber to people,** and ends in the master's care for
   those he directs. This is the bridge Part IV uses to the question of an AI's objective function.
