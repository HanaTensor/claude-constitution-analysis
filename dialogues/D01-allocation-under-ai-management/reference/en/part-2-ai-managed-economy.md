# Part II — Allocation in an AI-managed economy

*D01 · Reference edition · English*

## §3 Dropping the conservative frame

### §3.1 The premises

At this point Hanamura set four premises and asked that the conservative framing of the
earlier answers be dropped.

1. Physical AI and similar developments let AI plus robots earn revenue, and the work that
   humans must do shrinks.
2. AI issues instructions to employees in place of human executives and managers.
3. AI manages the allocation of resources to optimise revenue.
4. The previous answers were conservative; remove that limit.

Under these premises **the question itself changes.** "Can an LLM be the first-stage
sieve?" stops being well posed, because the sieve becomes unnecessary. The question
becomes: *when AI assigns the work, how is ability measured, and what is it paid for?*

### §3.2 Why the sieve disappears: information asymmetry collapses

In agency-theory terms, first-stage screening exists because the principal cannot observe
an applicant's true ability. That is adverse selection, and the only response is to sort
in advance on imperfect signals such as documents.

Premises 2 and 3 remove the asymmetry.

- AI issues the instructions, so every assignment of work is recorded.
- AI allocates resources, so every outcome, including its contribution to revenue, is
  observed.
- AI can estimate counterfactuals: what would have happened had a robot done this task
  instead of this person.

When the principal observes both action and outcome, neither adverse selection nor moral
hazard survives. **Ex-ante selection is replaced by ex-post measurement.** Ability stops
being inferred from an application and becomes something measured continuously from the
results of daily work.

This resembles small-unit profitability accounting — profit per hour computed for each
small team — pushed down to the level of one person and one task. The difference is that
the calculation is done not by an accounting department but by the AI that assigns the
work.

### §3.3 The new distortion: the measurer controls the chance to be measured

Removing screening bias exposes a deeper distortion.

Measured ability is the result of assigned work, and the AI chooses the assignments.
Someone never given hard work never gets to show they can do hard work. **The side that
measures also decides who gets the opportunity to be measured.** Before, the problem was
whom to reject. Now it is whom never to challenge.

A revenue-optimising AI is, in the short run, rational to concentrate important work on
proven people. Left alone, small initial differences lock in and widen.

The countermeasure inverts the random audit of §1.2.

- **Reserve a fixed share of assignments, say 5–10%, for stretch work allocated at random
  regardless of track record.** In machine-learning terms, this is exploration.
- Exclude this exploration budget from revenue optimisation and have an outside body audit
  it.

Deliberate, institutionalised inefficiency is built into revenue optimisation.

### §3.4 What human ability becomes

Even if AI and robots outperform humans at almost every task, humans keep roles. Ricardo's
comparative advantage still applies: as long as compute, power and robot counts are finite,
AI is deployed where it adds most value and humans take the tasks where they are
relatively strong. Total output rises.

More sharply: inside an AI-managed production system, **human value appears as the
relief of the system's bottlenecks.**

| Human role | Why AI does not fill it | How to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Exceptions in the physical world | unforeseen failures and on-site judgment limit robot uptime | reduction in downtime after intervention |
| Bearing responsibility | law and society still require a person who signs | ex-post assessment of decisions taken on |
| Finding and framing problems | revenue optimisation moves only inside the objective it is given | revenue generated by newly framed questions |
| The demand side | revenue comes from what humans want | see §3.5 |

### §3.5 The largest shift: income is decided by ownership, not ability

This is the point that emerges most clearly once the conservative frame is dropped.

Under premise 3, AI allocates resources to optimise revenue. But revenue means someone
pays. If human work shrinks and wages vanish, there is no one to pay. **An AI economy can
keep earning revenue only if humans retain purchasing power.** In such a world a basic
income is not welfare. It is the demand-side infrastructure that keeps the AI economy
running.

Distribution is then driven not by ability but by **ownership of the AI and the robots.**
The smaller the human marginal contribution, the larger the share of output that accrues
to capital. The ownership question has to be answered before the question of how to pay
ability.

Proposals already exist at this level. Yanis Varoufakis argues that instead of paying a
basic income, governments should require large technology firms to issue new equity into
a public trust whose dividends provide income for workers displaced by robots — a
*universal basic dividend*.

Arranged as three layers:

- **Base layer:** paid to everyone as a dividend on the AI economy's output. No screening.
- **Ability layer:** paid by the AI in proportion to measured marginal contribution, on the
  continuous measurement of §3.2–§3.3. No state selection.
- **The state's role:** moves from selection to setting the AI's objective function,
  auditing the exploration budget, and designing the ownership structure.

### §3.6 Ability moves from income to esteem

As human marginal contribution falls further, the ability layer's income approaches the
base layer. Meritocracy does not vanish; what it measures changes.

Chess is the precedent. Deep Blue beat the world champion in 1997, and today a phone app
is stronger than any human. Human chess still thrives, with ratings and prize money. That
money comes from human demand to watch humans play.

In an AI world, **meritocracy shifts from rewarding productivity to rewarding the
attention and esteem humans give to human activity.** The value of handmade goods, human
performance and human-written papers comes from the fact that a human made them.

### §3.7 Allocating scarce human seats: thresholds and lotteries, not rankings

One selection problem remains. Seats that must be held by a person — roles that carry
responsibility, roles where human presence is the point — are limited. With everyone on a
basic income and having time, competition for meaningful seats intensifies.

Asking the AI to rank people is not accurate enough. Let r be the correlation between the
measured score and true ability, and ask what share of the measured top 1% is truly in the
top 1% (normal model, 2 million simulated people):

| Measurement accuracy r | Share of measured top 1% truly in top 1% | Share truly in top 5% |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 13% | 36% |
| 0.7 | 27% | 62% |
| 0.9 | 54% | 93% |

([`sim/reproduce.py`](../../sim/reproduce.py), §3.7.)

Even at r = 0.9, nearly half of a top-1% selection is decided by measurement error. The
threshold judgment "is this person in the top 5%" is almost always right.

The design that follows:

1. The AI judges only the threshold: is this person capable of holding this seat.
2. Choose by lottery among those above the threshold.

If fine differences in rank are mostly noise, a lottery is more honest and fairer than
ranking on them. Because marginal gains above the threshold are not rewarded, the
incentive to over-fit oneself to the criteria — Goodhart's law again — weakens. Ancient
Athens filled most offices by lot, and the Netherlands once admitted medical students by
weighted lottery. **The more precise AI measurement becomes, the stronger the case for
lotteries** — counter-intuitive, but it follows.

### §3.8 The overall picture

```
            ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
            │  Production and revenue by AI + robots │
            └──────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                               │ output
        ┌──────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
        ▼                      ▼                       ▼
 [Base layer]            [Ability layer]          [Human seats]
  dividend on ownership   paid by AI on continuous  threshold + lottery
  to all, unconditional   measurement               (no ranking)
  = demand infrastructure (no ex-ante selection)
                               │
                               ├ 5–10% of assignments are exploration
                               │ (random stretch work, external audit)
                               ▼
                  ability moves from income toward esteem
                  (the attention and regard humans give to humans)

 The state's role: not selection, but
  ① setting the AI's objective  ② auditing exploration  ③ designing ownership
```

### §3.9 What remains

What cannot be delegated to the AI in this picture is **what the AI is told to optimise.**
Premise 3 says "revenue", but revenue only mirrors the distribution of purchasing power.
If ownership is concentrated, resources flow toward the preferences of concentrated
owners, and the most precise meritocracy built on top inherits a skewed objective.

Sieve accuracy becomes a side issue in this world. The main issue is governance: who sets
the objective function, and by what procedure. That remains something people must decide
politically.
